# Section 03 — Valuation

Valuation is the process of estimating the intrinsic value of a business. The gap between intrinsic value and market price is the investment opportunity. Understanding how to value a business — and the limitations of each method — is the core skill of fundamental investing.

---

## The Fundamental Principle

**Price is what you pay. Value is what you get.** — Warren Buffett

The market is a voting machine in the short run and a weighing machine in the long run. Valuation tries to find what the business "weighs" regardless of what votes it's currently getting.

---

## Valuation Approaches

| Approach | Method | Core Logic |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| [DCF Valuation](dcf-valuation.md) | Discount future cash flows at WACC | Intrinsic value = PV of all future cash flows |
| [Relative Valuation](relative-valuation.md) | Compare multiples to peers | Value = what the market pays for similar businesses |
| [Margin of Safety](margin-of-safety.md) | Buy at a discount to intrinsic value | Protect against estimation error and adverse outcomes |

---

## Why Use Multiple Methods

No single valuation method is definitive. Each has strengths and limitations:

- **DCF** is theoretically correct but extremely sensitive to assumptions. Small changes in growth rate or discount rate produce large value swings. It's a precision tool wielded with imprecise inputs.
- **Relative valuation** (comps) anchors to current market prices — which may themselves be misvalued. If the entire sector is overvalued, comps will tell you a stock is "fairly valued" relative to peers even if everyone is expensive.
- **Margin of safety** is not a valuation method per se, but a framework for acting on your valuation — it tells you how large a discount you need before the upside/downside is compelling.

Use at least two methods and triangulate. If DCF and comps both point to similar value, confidence is higher. If they diverge, understand why.

---

## The Role of Qualitative Judgment

Numbers are an output of assumptions, and assumptions require qualitative judgment. Before building any model:

1. **Understand the business model** — how does it make money, and why do customers pay?
2. **Assess the moat** — can it sustain margins and returns?
3. **Evaluate management** — will capital be deployed wisely?
4. **Understand the industry** — is the business in a growing or declining industry?

Garbage in, garbage out. A precise DCF built on wrong assumptions is worse than a rough estimate built on correct insights.

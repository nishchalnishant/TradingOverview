# Profitability Ratios

Profitability ratios measure how effectively a company converts revenue, assets, and equity into earnings. They are the primary lens for assessing business quality and competitive position.

---

## Margin Ratios

Margins measure profitability at different stages of the income statement. Each successive margin strips away another layer of costs.

### Gross Margin

**Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue**

What it measures: Pricing power and production/sourcing efficiency. The spread between what you charge and what it costs to make/deliver the product.

| Level | Interpretation |
|-------|---------------|
| >60% | Typically software, luxury goods, pharmaceuticals — high pricing power |
| 40–60% | Consumer branded goods, services, medical devices |
| 20–40% | Manufacturing, specialty retail |
| <20% | Distribution, basic manufacturing, grocery retail |

**Why it matters:** Gross margin sets the ceiling for every other margin metric. A 20% gross margin business cannot sustainably achieve a 15% net margin — all operating expenses must fit within the gross margin.

Gross margin stability or expansion over time signals **pricing power and/or improving scale**. Contracting gross margins signal input cost inflation, competitive pricing pressure, or a deteriorating product mix.

### Operating Margin (EBIT Margin)

**Operating Margin = EBIT / Revenue = Operating Income / Revenue**

What it measures: Core business profitability before the effects of capital structure (debt) and taxes. Best metric for comparing businesses with different leverage or tax situations.

**Operating Leverage:** The relationship between fixed costs and the sensitivity of operating margin to revenue changes.

- High fixed costs + low variable costs = high operating leverage. Revenue growth drops through to operating income at a high rate; revenue decline hits operating income hard.
- Example: A SaaS company with high fixed R&D and S&M costs can grow margins substantially as revenue scales — incremental revenue has near-100% gross margins once base costs are covered.

### Net Margin

**Net Margin = Net Income / Revenue**

What it measures: After-all-costs profitability — the percentage of each revenue dollar that becomes earnings for shareholders.

Net margin is significantly influenced by leverage (interest expense) and tax rate — factors not related to operational performance. Use operating margin for business quality comparisons; net margin for checking the overall economics of equity ownership.

**Typical Ranges:**

| Industry | Net Margin Range |
|----------|----------------|
| Software (mature) | 15–35% |
| Pharmaceuticals | 15–25% |
| Consumer staples | 8–15% |
| Healthcare services | 5–12% |
| Industrials | 5–10% |
| Retail | 2–6% |
| Grocery / Distribution | 1–3% |

## Return Ratios

Return ratios measure how much profit the company generates relative to the capital used to run the business. These are the most important metrics for assessing long-term business quality.

### Return on Equity (ROE)

**ROE = Net Income / Average Shareholders' Equity**

What it measures: How much profit is generated per dollar of shareholders' equity. The primary return metric from an equity investor's perspective.

**Interpretation:**
- ROE >15% consistently: High-quality business with pricing power or an asset-light model
- ROE 10–15%: Decent business; return roughly in line with cost of equity
- ROE <10%: Either a low-quality business, a capital-intensive industry, or a company in distress

**Warning:** High ROE can be manufactured by leverage. A company with $1 of equity and $9 of debt generating $0.15 of net income has a 15% ROE — but this is extremely fragile, not impressive.

### DuPont Decomposition

The three-factor DuPont model decomposes ROE into its fundamental drivers:

**ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier**

| Factor | Formula | Driver |
|--------|---------|--------|
| Net Profit Margin | Net Income / Revenue | Profitability |
| Asset Turnover | Revenue / Total Assets | Efficiency (capital productivity) |
| Equity Multiplier | Total Assets / Shareholders' Equity | Financial leverage |

**Five-Factor DuPont (more granular):**

**ROE = (EBIT/Revenue) × (Revenue/Assets) × (Assets/Equity) × (EBT/EBIT) × (Net Income/EBT)**

Breaking down further:
- EBIT/Revenue = Operating margin
- Revenue/Assets = Asset turnover
- Assets/Equity = Leverage
- EBT/EBIT = Interest burden (lower if high interest expense)
- Net Income/EBT = Tax burden

**Example — two companies with 20% ROE:**

| Driver | Company A (Software) | Company B (Retailer) |
|--------|---------------------|---------------------|
| Net Margin | 25% | 3% |
| Asset Turnover | 0.8x | 3.0x |
| Equity Multiplier | 1.0x | 2.2x |
| **ROE** | **20%** | **20%** |

Company A achieves its ROE through high margins with no leverage — durable and low-risk. Company B needs high asset utilization and leverage — vulnerable to margin compression or revenue decline.

### Return on Assets (ROA)

**ROA = Net Income / Average Total Assets**

What it measures: How efficiently the company uses all its assets (debt and equity financed) to generate profit. Less susceptible to leverage distortion than ROE.

ROA is particularly useful for comparing capital-intensive businesses (banks, manufacturers, utilities) where differences in leverage make ROE comparisons misleading.

**Typical benchmarks:** ROA >5% is generally good; >10% is exceptional. Banks typically target ROA of 1–1.5% (but use high leverage to achieve >10% ROE).

### Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)

**ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital**

Where:
- **NOPAT** = Net Operating Profit After Tax = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate)
- **Invested Capital** = Total Equity + Total Debt − Cash (or: Net PP&E + Net Working Capital + Other Operating Assets)

What it measures: The return on all capital deployed in the operating business — the purest measure of business economics because it excludes non-operating assets (excess cash) and adjusts out the tax shield from debt.

**Why ROIC is the gold standard:**
ROIC vs. WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital) is the key value creation test:
- **ROIC > WACC:** Each dollar reinvested creates value. This is the definition of a compounding machine.
- **ROIC < WACC:** The business destroys value when it invests or grows. Growth is actually value-destructive.

Companies with consistently high ROIC (>15–20%) over long periods are almost always competitively advantaged businesses. The durability of ROIC is as important as its level.

**Businesses with sustainably high ROIC:** Visa, Moody's, MSCI, Copart, Fastenal — all share pricing power and/or capital-light models.

## How to Interpret and Compare Profitability Ratios

### Peer Comparison

Never compare absolute margins or returns across industries without context:
- A 5% net margin is excellent for a grocery chain and terrible for a software company.
- 10% ROE is below average for a tech company and above average for a capital-intensive manufacturer.

**Rank within sector:** Is the company in the top quartile of its peer group? Consistently above-median profitability suggests competitive advantage.

### Historical Trends

Track 5–10 year history:
- **Stable high margins:** Pricing power is durable (moat exists)
- **Improving margins over time:** Operating leverage kicking in, or cost structure improving
- **Declining margins:** Competitive pressure, cost inflation, or business model deterioration

### What Drives High Profitability

| Driver | Mechanism | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| Pricing power (brand/moat) | Revenue grows without proportional COGS increase | LVMH, Apple |
| Asset-light model | Revenue generates from minimal fixed assets | Software, asset managers |
| Operating leverage | Fixed cost base allows margin expansion at scale | Cloud platforms |
| Cost leadership | Structural cost advantage vs. peers | Costco, Amazon logistics |
| Barriers to entry | Limited competition preserves margins | Visa/Mastercard networks |

### What Drives Low Profitability

| Driver | Mechanism | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| Commodity products | No pricing power, compete on price | Airlines, commodity chemicals |
| Capital intensity | High CapEx absorbs cash flow | Utilities, steel mills |
| Intense competition | Race to the bottom on price | Grocery retail |
| Pass-through cost model | Revenue moves with input costs, thin margin | Distributors, refiners |
| High leverage | Interest expense consumes operating income | Leveraged buyout targets |

# Liquidity and Leverage Ratios

Liquidity ratios measure a company's ability to meet short-term obligations. Leverage ratios measure the extent of debt in the capital structure and the company's capacity to service it. Together, they define financial health and distress risk.

---

## Liquidity Ratios

Liquidity ratios assess whether the company can pay bills due within the next 12 months. They compare current assets (assets expected to convert to cash soon) against current liabilities (obligations due soon).

### Current Ratio

**Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities**

What it measures: The most basic liquidity test. For every $1 of near-term obligations, how much in current assets does the company have?

**Interpretation:**

| Current Ratio | Interpretation |
|--------------|---------------|
| <1.0 | Current liabilities exceed current assets — potential short-term liquidity issue |
| 1.0–1.5 | Adequate but tight; acceptable for businesses with predictable cash flow |
| 1.5–2.5 | Comfortable; common for healthy manufacturing and retail businesses |
| >3.0 | Highly liquid; may also indicate inefficient use of capital (excess idle assets) |

**Context matters:** A current ratio of 0.8 is alarming for a manufacturer but normal for a large retailer (Walmart consistently runs below 1.0 because it has strong cash flows and pays suppliers slowly while collecting from customers immediately). Subscription businesses can also run low current ratios when deferred revenue inflates current liabilities.

### Quick Ratio (Acid-Test Ratio)

**Quick Ratio = (Cash + Short-term Investments + Accounts Receivable) / Current Liabilities**

Or equivalently:

**Quick Ratio = (Current Assets − Inventory − Prepaid Expenses) / Current Liabilities**

What it measures: A more conservative liquidity test. It excludes inventory (which may not be easily converted to cash at full value) and prepaid expenses (which cannot be converted to cash at all).

**Why more useful than current ratio:**
For inventory-heavy businesses (retailers, manufacturers), inventory may take months to sell and may need to be marked down. The quick ratio gives a stress-tested view of short-term solvency.

| Quick Ratio | Interpretation |
|------------|---------------|
| <0.5 | Tight liquidity; reliant on inventory liquidation or credit lines |
| 0.5–1.0 | Acceptable for businesses with predictable inventory turns |
| >1.0 | Comfortable near-term liquidity even without selling inventory |

### Cash Ratio

**Cash Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents + Short-term Investments) / Current Liabilities**

The most conservative liquidity test. It asks: could the company pay all current liabilities today, using only its most liquid assets?

In practice, a cash ratio of 1.0 or above would represent massive excess cash that most companies don't carry. This ratio is most useful in distress analysis to determine how close to insolvency a company is.

## Leverage Ratios

Leverage ratios measure the amount of debt in the capital structure and whether the company can service its debt comfortably.

### Debt-to-Equity (D/E) Ratio

**D/E = Total Debt / Shareholders' Equity**

Or sometimes calculated as:

**Net D/E = Net Debt / Shareholders' Equity** where Net Debt = Total Debt − Cash

What it measures: How much of the company's financing comes from creditors vs. equity owners. Higher D/E means more leverage.

**Interpretation:**

| D/E Ratio | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------------|
| 0–0.3x | Conservative capital structure; financial flexibility |
| 0.3–1.0x | Moderate leverage; common for most healthy businesses |
| 1.0–3.0x | Higher leverage; common in capital-intensive industries |
| >3.0x | High leverage; acceptable only with stable, predictable cash flows (utilities, REITs) |

**Note:** D/E varies enormously by industry. Banks and insurance companies have extremely high D/E by design (deposits are technically liabilities). Real estate and utilities routinely carry D/E of 2–4x. Technology companies often have net cash (negative net debt). Always compare within industry.

**Negative D/E:** If a company has massive buybacks and negative book equity (e.g., McDonald's), D/E is mathematically negative — the ratio breaks down. Use Net Debt/EBITDA instead.

### Net Debt / EBITDA

**Net Debt/EBITDA = (Total Debt − Cash) / EBITDA**

The most commonly used leverage metric in credit analysis and M&A. It measures how many years of pre-tax, pre-depreciation earnings it would take to repay all net debt.

**Interpretation:**

| Net Debt/EBITDA | Interpretation |
|----------------|---------------|
| <0x (Net Cash) | No leverage; company holds more cash than debt |
| 0–1.5x | Conservative; substantial financial flexibility |
| 1.5–3.0x | Moderate; comfortable for most investment-grade businesses |
| 3.0–5.0x | Higher leverage; typical for LBOs, some capital-intensive industries |
| >5.0x | High leverage; acceptable only with very stable cash flows; approaching distress territory for cyclicals |

**Covenant thresholds:** Leveraged buyouts and high-yield bonds often have maintenance covenants requiring Net Debt/EBITDA to stay below 5.0–6.0x. Breaching covenants can trigger loan acceleration.

**EBITDA caveat:** EBITDA excludes CapEx. For capital-intensive businesses, use Net Debt/FCF or Net Debt/(EBITDA − CapEx) for a more realistic picture of debt repayment capacity.

### Debt-to-Capital Ratio

**Debt-to-Capital = Total Debt / (Total Debt + Total Equity)**

Measures the percentage of the capital structure that is debt-financed. Ranges from 0% (all equity) to 100% (all debt). Most healthy companies target 20–50% debt-to-capital.

### Total Debt / Total Assets

**Total Debt / Total Assets = Total Liabilities / Total Assets**

What fraction of total assets is financed by creditors. A complement to D/E that sidesteps the book equity distortion problem. A ratio approaching 1.0 means the company is nearly insolvent on a book basis.

## Debt Service Coverage Ratios

### Interest Coverage Ratio (ICR)

**Interest Coverage = EBIT / Interest Expense**

Or (more commonly used in credit analysis):

**Interest Coverage = EBITDA / Interest Expense**

What it measures: How many times the company could pay its interest expense from current earnings. The primary measure of near-term ability to service debt.

**Interpretation:**

| Interest Coverage | Interpretation |
|------------------|---------------|
| <1.5x | Danger zone; earnings barely cover interest; distress risk |
| 1.5–3.0x | Tight; vulnerable to earnings decline |
| 3–5x | Adequate coverage for moderately leveraged businesses |
| 5–10x | Comfortable; typical for investment-grade companies |
| >10x | Very conservative leverage or minimal debt |

**Fixed Charge Coverage Ratio (FCCR):**
More comprehensive than interest coverage — includes lease payments and required debt principal payments:

`FCCR = (EBIT + Lease Payments) / (Interest Expense + Lease Payments + Debt Principal/(1−Tax Rate))`

## Assessing Financial Health: Integrated Framework

When combining these ratios for a holistic assessment:

**Step 1: Check short-term liquidity first**
- Current ratio and quick ratio — can the company pay bills this year?
- Check size of credit facilities / revolving credit access (in the notes) as backup liquidity

**Step 2: Assess leverage load**
- Net Debt/EBITDA vs. industry norms
- Trend in net debt: rising or falling over past 3–5 years?

**Step 3: Check debt service capacity**
- EBITDA interest coverage
- FCF coverage of total debt service (interest + amortization)

**Step 4: Check maturity profile**
- When does the debt mature? Near-term maturities in a tight credit market = risk
- Is debt fixed or floating rate? Floating rate debt means interest expense rises with rate hikes

**Step 5: Compare to credit ratings**
Investment grade (BBB− and above) vs. sub-investment grade (BB+ and below) reflects how the market prices distress risk. Falling toward sub-investment grade triggers forced selling by many institutional holders.

## Predicting Financial Distress: Altman Z-Score

The Altman Z-Score is a multi-factor model that predicts bankruptcy probability.

**Z = 1.2(X1) + 1.4(X2) + 3.3(X3) + 0.6(X4) + 1.0(X5)**

Where:
- X1 = Working Capital / Total Assets
- X2 = Retained Earnings / Total Assets
- X3 = EBIT / Total Assets
- X4 = Market Value of Equity / Total Liabilities
- X5 = Revenue / Total Assets

**Zones:**
| Z-Score | Interpretation |
|---------|---------------|
| >2.99 | "Safe" zone — low bankruptcy risk |
| 1.81–2.99 | "Grey" zone — uncertain |
| <1.81 | "Distress" zone — high bankruptcy risk |

The Z-Score was developed on manufacturing companies; modified versions exist for private companies and non-manufacturing businesses. Use as a screening tool, not a definitive judgment.

# Industry and Sector Analysis

Before analyzing an individual company, understand the industry it operates in. A mediocre company in an excellent industry can perform well; a great company in a structurally terrible industry will fight headwinds forever. Industry analysis sets the context for every company-specific judgment.

---

## Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter's Five Forces framework (1979) is the standard model for assessing industry attractiveness. The five forces collectively determine average industry profitability — industries where all five forces are favorable generate persistently high margins; industries where multiple forces are adverse generate persistently low margins.

### Force 1: Threat of New Entrants

**Definition:** How easily can new competitors enter the market and take share?

High barriers to entry protect existing players. Low barriers invite perpetual competitive pressure.

**Barriers to entry:**
- High capital requirements (steel mills, semiconductor fabs, airlines)
- Economies of scale (new entrants can't match incumbent cost structure)
- Network effects (users already on incumbent platforms)
- Regulatory barriers (banking licenses, pharmaceutical approvals, broadcast licenses)
- Proprietary technology or IP (patents, trade secrets)
- Brand and customer loyalty
- Access to distribution (retail shelf space, preferred vendor status)

**Assessment questions:**
- How long would it take a well-capitalized entrant to establish a viable competing business?
- Has the industry seen successful new entrants in the last 10 years?
- Are there structural cost advantages that incumbents have and entrants lack?

### Force 2: Bargaining Power of Suppliers

**Definition:** How much pricing power do the company's input suppliers hold?

Powerful suppliers can raise prices, reduce quality, or limit availability — compressing margins.

**Supplier power is high when:**
- Few suppliers of a critical input exist (ASML for EUV lithography equipment)
- Switching to an alternative supplier is costly or slow
- Suppliers compete in a concentrated market (oligopoly)
- The input represents a large % of the buyer's total cost

**Supplier power is low when:**
- Many substitutable suppliers exist (commodity inputs)
- The buyer represents a large % of the supplier's revenue
- Forward integration by the buyer is feasible

**Investment implication:** Companies with high supplier power face margin pressure when commodity inputs spike (airlines with jet fuel, food companies with agricultural inputs). Companies with low supplier power (software makers, brand companies) are largely insulated.

### Force 3: Bargaining Power of Buyers

**Definition:** How much pricing power do the company's customers hold?

Powerful buyers push prices down and demand better terms — compressing margins.

**Buyer power is high when:**
- Buyers are large and concentrated (a few major customers = high concentration risk)
- Products are undifferentiated (commodities)
- Switching costs are low
- Buyers have backward integration options (building in-house vs. buying)
- Buyers have excellent price visibility and comparison ability

**Buyer power is low when:**
- Many fragmented buyers exist
- High switching costs exist
- Products are differentiated and mission-critical
- Buyer spends represent a small % of their total budget (so price is less scrutinized)

**Investment implication:** Software vendors embedded in enterprise workflows (SAP, Salesforce) have low buyer power because switching is expensive and disruptive. Commodity goods manufacturers selling to large retailers have high buyer power (Walmart squeezes suppliers relentlessly).

### Force 4: Threat of Substitutes

**Definition:** How easily can customers switch to an alternative product or service that meets the same need, even if it's from a different industry?

Substitutes constrain pricing and can erode demand.

**Examples of substitution:**
- Train vs. plane for short-haul travel
- Streaming vs. cable TV
- Email vs. postal mail
- Plant-based protein vs. conventional meat
- Solar energy vs. natural gas generation

**Assessment questions:**
- Does the substitute perform the same core function at a competitive price/performance ratio?
- Are switching costs to the substitute high or low?
- Is the substitute improving (declining cost curves) or stable?

**Investment implication:** Industries facing rapid technological substitution (print media, traditional retail, landline telecom) are structurally declining. The substitute can be from a completely different sector.

### Force 5: Competitive Rivalry Among Existing Firms

**Definition:** How intensely do existing competitors fight for market share?

High rivalry drives down prices, increases marketing spend, and compresses margins.

**Rivalry is intense when:**
- Many similarly sized competitors exist (no dominant player)
- Industry growth is slow (companies fight for share in a fixed pie)
- Products are undifferentiated (price is the primary basis of competition)
- Fixed costs are high and exit barriers are high (companies need to keep producing even when unprofitable)
- Competitors have diverse strategies and goals

**Rivalry is moderate or low when:**
- One or two dominant players control the market (Visa/Mastercard in payments)
- Industry is growing fast (room for all to grow without fighting for share)
- Strong differentiation reduces direct head-to-head competition
- Industry norms (rational pricing behavior, no price wars)

**Summary — Five Forces Assessment Grid:**

| Force | Impact on Industry | Key Question |
|-------|------------------|--------------|
| New Entrants | High threat → low margins | How hard to enter? |
| Supplier Power | High power → cost pressure | How many suppliers exist? |
| Buyer Power | High power → price pressure | How concentrated are buyers? |
| Substitutes | High threat → demand ceiling | What else meets the need? |
| Rivalry | High rivalry → price war | How many competitors, how differentiated? |

**Best industries to be in:** All five forces are favorable — difficult to enter, buyers are fragmented, no substitutes, rational rivalry. Payment networks, rating agencies, stock exchanges.

**Worst industries to be in:** All forces adverse — airlines (high capital, powerful suppliers [Boeing, oil], powerful buyers [corporate travel managers], substitutes [video conferencing], brutal rivalry).

## Industry Life Cycle

Industries evolve through predictable stages. Investment strategy should match the stage.

### Stage 1: Emergence / Introduction

**Characteristics:**
- Product just developed; technology unproven
- Few customers; high education/awareness costs
- Losses typical; cash burn high
- Multiple competing standards/technologies

**Investment characteristics:** Extreme risk; potential multi-bagger returns. Most companies fail. Binary outcomes. For venture capital, not typically for public equity value investors unless a clear winner has emerged.

**Examples (historical):** Early internet (mid-1990s), early biotech (1980s), early EVs (2010–2015).

### Stage 2: Growth

**Characteristics:**
- Technology accepted; demand accelerating
- Revenue growing 20–50%+ annually
- Profitability emerging but margins still building
- Competitive shakeout beginning

**Investment characteristics:** Growth investors dominate. Valuations are high on trailing metrics but potentially reasonable on forward earnings. Key question: will the company be a winner or loser in the consolidation?

**Key metrics:** Revenue growth, gross margin trajectory, customer acquisition cost vs. lifetime value (SaaS), market share gains.

**Examples (recent):** Cloud computing (2012–2020), cybersecurity (2015–2023), GLP-1 drugs (2023–present).

### Stage 3: Maturity / Consolidation

**Characteristics:**
- Industry growth decelerating to GDP-level rates
- A few dominant players have emerged
- High profitability for leaders; weak margins for laggards
- Focus shifts from growth to efficiency and return of capital

**Investment characteristics:** Value and quality investors. Dividends and buybacks meaningful. Key question: does the leader have a moat that sustains margins, or will margins compress?

**Examples:** Consumer staples, payment networks, enterprise software incumbents, healthcare equipment.

### Stage 4: Decline

**Characteristics:**
- Structural demand shrinkage (technology disruption, regulatory change, demographic shift)
- Revenue declining
- Capacity reduction; weak players exit
- Remaining players compete for a shrinking pie

**Investment characteristics:** Value traps are common here. Occasional "melting ice cube" plays (generating cash while declining, returning it to shareholders) can work, but require strict discipline. Avoid companies with heavy debt in declining industries.

**Examples:** Newspapers, physical retail, traditional telecom (landlines), coal.

## Sector Rotation and Macro Sensitivity

Different sectors have different sensitivity to economic cycles, interest rates, and inflation. Understanding these linkages is the foundation of top-down investing. See [sector-rotation.md](../05-macro-and-sector/sector-rotation.md) for detailed cycle mapping.

**Cyclical sectors** (performance tied to economic growth):
- Materials, Energy, Industrials, Consumer Discretionary, Technology (hardware)

**Defensive sectors** (stable regardless of economic cycle):
- Consumer Staples, Healthcare, Utilities, Telecom

**Interest rate sensitive sectors:**
- Utilities, REITs, and bond-proxy dividend stocks decline when rates rise (discount rate effect)
- Financials (banks) tend to benefit from rising rates (wider net interest margins)
- High-multiple growth stocks are more sensitive to rate increases (long-duration equity)

## Assessing Industry Attractiveness: Summary Checklist

Before selecting stocks in an industry, answer:

- [ ] Are barriers to entry high? What are they?
- [ ] Is the industry structurally growing, stable, or declining?
- [ ] Is pricing rational? Have there been price wars historically?
- [ ] What is the typical ROIC for industry leaders? Is it above the cost of capital?
- [ ] What is the power balance between suppliers, companies, and customers?
- [ ] Is there a credible technological substitute on the horizon?
- [ ] At what stage is the industry life cycle?
- [ ] Is this a good industry at all stages, or only at the current macro moment?

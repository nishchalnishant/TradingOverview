# Income Statement

The income statement (also called the P&L — profit and loss) reports a company's revenues, expenses, and profits over a specific period. It answers the question: **did the business make money, and how much?**

---

## Structure: Top to Bottom

```
Revenue (Net Sales)
− Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)
= Gross Profit
− Operating Expenses (SG&A, R&D)
= Operating Income (EBIT)
± Non-operating items (interest income/expense, other)
= Pre-tax Income (EBT)
− Income Taxes
= Net Income
÷ Diluted Shares Outstanding
= Diluted EPS
```

---

## Line-by-Line Breakdown

### Revenue (Net Sales)

The top line — total sales recognized in the period, net of returns, discounts, and allowances.

- **Product companies:** Recognized when goods are shipped or delivered (control transferred).
- **Service companies:** Recognized as services are performed (percentage-of-completion or milestone-based).
- **Subscription companies:** Recognized ratably over the subscription period, regardless of when cash is received.

Always check whether revenue is growing organically (volume + price) or inorganically (acquisitions). Acquisitions inflate reported revenue without improving the underlying business.

### Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

Direct costs attributable to producing the goods or services sold. Includes raw materials, direct labor, and manufacturing overhead for product companies; direct service delivery costs for service companies.

**Gross Profit = Revenue − COGS**

**Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue**

Gross margin is a proxy for pricing power and production efficiency. It should be relatively stable unless input costs change or the company faces pricing pressure.

### Operating Expenses

Costs not directly tied to production:

- **SG&A (Selling, General & Administrative):** Salaries, marketing, rent, management overhead.
- **R&D (Research & Development):** Investment in future products. GAAP requires expensing most R&D immediately (unlike CapEx), which depresses earnings for technology and pharma companies.
- **Depreciation & Amortization (D&A):** Non-cash charge for the wear of assets (depreciation) and amortization of intangibles (often from acquisitions). Reported either in COGS or operating expenses depending on the asset.

### Operating Income (EBIT)

**EBIT = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses**

EBIT (Earnings Before Interest and Taxes) is the core measure of operating profitability — how much the business earns from its operations before capital structure (debt) and tax choices distort the picture. It is the primary metric for comparing profitability across companies with different leverage.

**Operating Margin = EBIT / Revenue**

### Non-Operating Items

Items below the operating line that affect earnings but not core business performance:

- **Interest expense:** Cost of debt financing. Higher debt → lower net income, even with identical EBIT.
- **Interest income:** Return on cash holdings.
- **Equity method income:** Share of earnings from minority-owned subsidiaries.
- **Gains/losses on asset sales:** One-time items; should be excluded from normalized earnings.
- **Foreign currency gains/losses:** Can be large for multinational companies.

### Pre-Tax Income (EBT)

EBT = EBIT ± non-operating items. The tax is applied to this line.

### Net Income

**Net Income = EBT × (1 − Effective Tax Rate)**

The bottom line — but not the most important number. Net income is highly susceptible to accounting choices, one-time items, and tax timing differences. Always reconcile to operating cash flow.

### Earnings Per Share (EPS)

**Basic EPS = Net Income / Basic Shares Outstanding**

**Diluted EPS = Net Income / Diluted Shares Outstanding**

Diluted shares include options, warrants, and convertible instruments that could dilute existing shareholders. Always use diluted EPS for analysis. Companies with heavy stock-based compensation have meaningfully higher diluted share counts.

## Reading Trends: What to Analyze

### Revenue Growth

Look at year-over-year (YoY) growth rates across 5–10 years, not just the most recent period.

| Pattern | Implication |
|---------|-------------|
| Consistent 10–20%+ organic growth | Strong demand, expanding market, pricing power |
| Growth decelerating each year | Maturing business, increasing competition |
| Lumpy/irregular growth | Cyclical business, project-based revenue, or M&A-driven |
| Declining revenue | Structural headwinds or business model problems |

Revenue growth rate matters more than the absolute revenue number.

### Margin Trends

Track gross margin and operating margin over time:

- **Expanding margins:** Pricing power, operating leverage (fixed costs spread over more revenue), cost discipline.
- **Contracting margins:** Input cost inflation, pricing pressure, mix shift toward lower-margin products, investment for growth (acceptable if temporary).
- **Stable margins with growing revenue:** Classic scaling business — high quality.

**Operating leverage** means fixed costs are a high proportion of total costs. When revenue grows, a larger share drops to operating income. Software companies have extreme operating leverage; retail companies have little.

### EPS vs. Revenue Growth

If EPS grows faster than revenue for multiple years, this suggests:
1. Margin expansion (good)
2. Share buybacks reducing denominator (neutral to good)
3. Increasing leverage (potentially risky)

If revenue grows but EPS stagnates or falls, investigate: rising costs, dilution, or rising interest expense.

## Non-Recurring Items and Normalized Earnings

GAAP net income includes items that won't repeat. Reported EPS can be significantly distorted.

**Common non-recurring charges:**
- Restructuring charges (layoffs, facility closures)
- Impairment charges (goodwill or asset write-downs)
- Litigation settlements
- Gains/losses on divestitures
- Acquisition-related costs

**Normalized (Adjusted) EPS** adds back these items to show the run-rate earnings power. Management always presents adjusted EPS favorably; scrutinize what they exclude and whether the exclusions are truly one-time.

**Watch for "recurring non-recurring" charges:** Companies that take restructuring charges every year are effectively treating them as recurring costs. Normalize over multiple years to get a realistic picture.

### Stock-Based Compensation (SBC)

SBC is a real economic cost to shareholders (dilution) but is added back in many "adjusted" metrics. For companies with heavy SBC (many tech companies pay 5–15% of revenue in SBC), ignore it in valuations at your peril.

## Red Flags in the Income Statement

**1. Revenue growth far outpacing industry peers**
Could signal genuine market share gains — but also aggressive revenue recognition. Cross-check with receivables growth on the balance sheet. If receivables grow faster than revenue, the company may be pulling forward future revenue.

**2. Gross margin inconsistency across quarters**
Lumpy margins can indicate channel stuffing (shipping excess inventory to distributors to hit short-term revenue targets). Margins compress in the following quarter as returns come in or pricing resets.

**3. Earnings growing while cash flow is flat or declining**
The most important divergence. Net income depends on accruals; operating cash flow does not. Persistent earnings-cash divergence almost always resolves against the stock.

**4. Operating income growing but free cash flow stagnant**
If CapEx is rising alongside earnings, the company may need heavy reinvestment just to maintain its current performance — not a capital-light business.

**5. High reliance on non-operating income**
A company reporting strong EPS driven by gains on investments or asset sales, not by operating income growth, is masking operational weakness.

**6. Rapid change in effective tax rate**
A sudden drop in effective tax rate can boost EPS without any operational improvement. Check the footnotes: tax credits, deferred tax assets, or geographic income shifting. If non-recurring, normalize it out.

**7. Revenue concentration risk**
Not on the face of the income statement, but often in the footnotes: if one customer is >10% of revenue, that relationship carries significant risk.

## Quick Reference: Key Income Statement Metrics

| Metric | Formula | What It Tells You |
|--------|---------|------------------|
| Gross Margin | Gross Profit / Revenue | Production efficiency, pricing power |
| Operating Margin | EBIT / Revenue | Core business profitability |
| Net Margin | Net Income / Revenue | After all costs including taxes and interest |
| Revenue Growth (YoY) | (Rev_t − Rev_{t-1}) / Rev_{t-1} | Top-line momentum |
| EPS Growth | (EPS_t − EPS_{t-1}) / EPS_{t-1} | Bottom-line momentum per share |
| Operating Leverage | % change EBIT / % change Revenue | Sensitivity of earnings to revenue changes |

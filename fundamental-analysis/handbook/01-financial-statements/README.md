# Section 01 — Financial Statements

Financial statements are the primary data source for fundamental analysis. Every ratio, valuation model, and quality check derives from these three documents. Learning to read them critically — not just mechanically — is the foundation of the entire discipline.

---

## The Three Statements

| Statement | What It Measures | Time Frame |
|-----------|-----------------|------------|
| [Income Statement](income-statement.md) | Profitability — revenues, costs, and earnings | Period (quarter/year) |
| [Balance Sheet](balance-sheet.md) | Financial position — assets, liabilities, equity | Point in time (snapshot) |
| [Cash Flow Statement](cash-flow-statement.md) | Cash generation and usage | Period (quarter/year) |

---

## How They Connect

The three statements are deeply linked:

- **Net income** flows from the income statement into retained earnings on the balance sheet (via the equity section).
- **Depreciation & amortization** is a non-cash charge on the income statement; it feeds into the operating section of the cash flow statement as an add-back.
- **CapEx** appears as a cash outflow in investing activities on the cash flow statement and increases PP&E on the balance sheet.
- The **ending cash balance** on the cash flow statement must match cash on the balance sheet.
- **Working capital changes** (receivables, inventory, payables) bridge the income statement and balance sheet, and appear in operating cash flow.

Understanding these linkages helps you detect inconsistencies — the fastest signal that something is wrong.

---

## Reading Order

When analyzing a company for the first time:

1. **Start with the income statement** — get a sense of size, growth rate, and margin structure.
2. **Move to the cash flow statement** — check whether earnings translate to cash.
3. **Use the balance sheet** — assess the capital structure, financial health, and asset quality.
4. **Read the footnotes** — this is where management hides the complexity. Revenue recognition policies, pension liabilities, lease commitments, and contingencies all live here.
5. **Compare multiple years** — single-period analysis is nearly useless. You need trends.

---

## What to Focus On

**Income Statement:** Revenue growth trajectory, gross margin stability, operating leverage, one-time vs. recurring items.

**Balance Sheet:** Debt load relative to earnings power, asset quality (goodwill as a % of total assets), working capital dynamics.

**Cash Flow Statement:** Free cash flow (FCF = operating cash flow minus CapEx), cash conversion of earnings, financing needs.

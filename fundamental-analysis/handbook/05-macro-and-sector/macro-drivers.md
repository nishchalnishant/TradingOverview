# Macro Drivers

Macro forces are the environmental conditions in which all businesses operate. They don't dictate company-specific outcomes, but they create powerful tailwinds and headwinds that affect entire sectors, valuation multiples, and discount rates. Understanding them allows you to identify regime changes before they fully appear in financial statements.

---

## Interest Rates

Interest rates are the single most influential macro variable for asset valuations. They affect DCF valuations mathematically, and affect business economics through borrowing costs, consumer spending, and currency.

### How Interest Rates Affect DCF Valuations

The risk-free rate is the foundation of WACC. When rates rise:
- WACC increases
- The discount rate applied to future cash flows increases
- Present value of future cash flows decreases
- All stock valuations compress (all else equal)

**Duration sensitivity:** Long-duration assets (high-growth stocks with most value in distant future cash flows) are more sensitive to rate increases than short-duration assets (value stocks with cash flows concentrated in the near term). This is why rising rates particularly hurt high-multiple growth stocks.

**Example:** A company with P/E of 35x (most value 10+ years out) falls more than a company with P/E of 12x when rates rise 200bps. Same fundamental business; different sensitivity.

### How Interest Rates Affect Business Economics

| Effect | Impact on Companies |
|--------|-------------------|
| Higher borrowing costs | Increases interest expense for leveraged companies; reduces FCF |
| Reduces consumer spending | Consumers spend less on discretionary items as debt servicing rises (mortgages, auto loans, credit cards) |
| Strengthens the dollar | Reduces earnings of US multinationals (foreign revenue translates to fewer dollars) |
| Increases hurdle rates for M&A | Makes acquisitions harder to justify; deal multiples compress |
| Mortgage rate effect | Higher rates slow housing market → construction, home improvement, appliances decline |

### Rate-Sensitive Sectors

| Sector | Rate Rising | Rate Falling |
|--------|------------|-------------|
| Utilities | Negative (high-debt, bond proxy) | Positive |
| REITs | Negative (debt-heavy, bond proxy) | Positive |
| Banks | Positive (NIM widens) | Negative |
| Insurance | Positive (investment income rises) | Negative |
| Tech (high multiple) | Negative (long duration) | Positive |
| Industrials (leveraged) | Negative | Positive |
| Consumer Discretionary | Negative (debt-financed spending falls) | Positive |

### Reading Rates: Key Data Points

- **Federal Funds Rate:** The overnight benchmark rate set by the Federal Reserve. All other rates are influenced by this.
- **10-Year Treasury Yield:** The risk-free rate used in most DCF/WACC calculations. Watch for breakouts and trend changes.
- **2-Year/10-Year Yield Spread (Yield Curve):** Normal curve slopes upward. Inverted curve (2-year > 10-year) has historically preceded recessions by 6–18 months. One of the most reliable recession indicators.
- **Real Rate:** Nominal rate minus inflation. High real rates are more restrictive than high nominal rates in an inflationary environment.
- **SOFR / Credit Spreads:** Corporate high-yield (HY) spreads over Treasuries measure credit market stress. Widening spreads signal deteriorating credit conditions and rising distress risk.

## Inflation

Inflation measures the rate of increase in general price levels. It affects purchasing power, input costs, corporate margins, and central bank policy.

### Inflation's Impact on Corporate Earnings

**Pricing Power Test:** Inflation is the ultimate stress test for moats. Companies with genuine pricing power can pass cost increases through to customers, preserving margins. Companies without pricing power see margins compressed.

**Input cost inflation:** Raw materials (oil, agricultural commodities, metals), energy, and labor are the primary inputs. When they rise faster than output prices, gross margins compress.

**Operating expense inflation:** Wage inflation (tight labor markets) increases SG&A costs independent of revenue. Service businesses (restaurants, healthcare) are particularly vulnerable.

**Nominal Revenue Boost:** Inflation inflates nominal revenue even without real volume growth. A company selling the same number of units at 5% higher prices reports 5% revenue growth. This can flatter earnings temporarily — but only companies with pricing power can sustain this without volume loss.

### Inflation Regimes and Asset Classes

| Regime | Stock Implications |
|--------|------------------|
| Low, stable inflation (1–3%) | Goldilocks for equities; supports high multiples |
| Moderate rising inflation (3–5%) | Mixed; pricing power companies win, others lose |
| High inflation (>5%) | Rate hike pressure; multiple compression; value over growth |
| Deflation (<0%) | Dangerous; real debt burden rises; corporate revenues fall |

### Inflation Data to Monitor

**CPI (Consumer Price Index):** The primary measure of consumer inflation. Core CPI (ex-food and energy) is more stable and closely watched by the Fed.

**PPI (Producer Price Index):** Inflation at the production level. PPI leads CPI by months — input cost increases eventually pass through to consumer prices. PPI rising faster than CPI = margin compression risk.

**PCE (Personal Consumption Expenditures):** The Fed's preferred inflation measure. Weights categories by spending patterns, tends to run slightly below CPI.

**Breakeven Inflation (TIPS spread):** The market-implied expected inflation rate embedded in TIPS pricing vs. nominal Treasuries. Forward-looking.

## GDP Growth and the Economic Cycle

GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the total value of goods and services produced. Its growth rate drives corporate revenue growth at the aggregate level.

### The Four Stages of the Economic Cycle

**1. Expansion (Recovery)**
- GDP growing, unemployment falling
- Corporate revenues rising, earnings recovering
- Credit available and cheap
- Confidence rising
- Best sectors: Financials, Consumer Discretionary, Industrials, Technology

**2. Peak**
- GDP growth at maximum but decelerating
- Inflation rising; labor market tight
- Central bank tightening (raising rates)
- Earnings growth still positive but multiple compression begins
- Best sectors: Energy, Materials (inflation beneficiaries); start rotating to defensive

**3. Contraction (Recession)**
- GDP declining (two consecutive quarters = technical recession)
- Unemployment rising
- Corporate revenues and earnings falling
- Credit tightening; defaults rising
- Best sectors: Consumer Staples, Healthcare, Utilities (defensive)

**4. Trough (Bottom)**
- GDP at its lowest point; contraction slowing
- Unemployment at peak; confidence at bottom
- Central bank easing (cutting rates, QE)
- Earnings at trough; valuations look expensive on current (trough) earnings but cheap on forward (recovery) earnings
- Best sectors: Financials, Consumer Discretionary, Technology (early cycle beneficiaries)

## Central Bank Policy (Monetary Policy)

The Federal Reserve (US) and other central banks manage monetary conditions primarily through:

### Tools

**Federal Funds Rate Target:** The primary lever. Rate hikes slow the economy; rate cuts stimulate.

**Quantitative Easing (QE):** Central bank purchases of bonds (Treasuries, MBS) to inject liquidity and lower long-term rates. Expands the Fed's balance sheet. Used at the zero lower bound when rate cuts are exhausted.

**Quantitative Tightening (QT):** Reverse of QE — allowing bonds to mature without reinvestment (or actively selling). Drains liquidity; tightens financial conditions.

**Forward Guidance:** The Fed's communication about future policy intentions. Markets price the future path of rates (the "Fed dot plot"). Shifts in guidance can move markets significantly even without actual rate changes.

### Monetary Policy Transmission Channels

1. **Interest rate channel:** Lower rates → cheaper borrowing → more investment and spending
2. **Asset price channel:** Lower rates → higher stock/bond prices → wealth effect → more spending
3. **Credit channel:** Lower rates → banks lend more → businesses invest and consumers spend
4. **Exchange rate channel:** Lower rates → weaker dollar → exports more competitive → manufacturing benefits

### CME FedWatch Tool

Monitor market-implied probability of rate changes at upcoming FOMC meetings. Dramatic shifts in probabilities (e.g., from 20% to 70% chance of a cut) move markets before the actual decision.

## Fiscal Policy

Government taxation and spending decisions that affect aggregate demand.

**Expansionary fiscal policy:** Government increases spending or cuts taxes → stimulates demand → GDP growth support.

**Contractionary fiscal policy:** Government cuts spending or raises taxes → reduces demand → may slow growth.

**Investment implications:**
- Large fiscal stimulus boosts infrastructure, defense, healthcare, and other government-exposed sectors
- Tax cuts directly boost after-tax earnings (a 21% → 25% corporate tax rate increase reduces net income by ~5% mechanically)
- Government spending changes can create or destroy sector tailwinds (Infrastructure Investment Act → construction, materials; Inflation Reduction Act → clean energy, EVs)

## Key Macro Data to Monitor

Build a regular reading habit around these releases:

| Report | Frequency | What to Watch |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| CPI / Core CPI | Monthly | Year-over-year change; month-over-month acceleration/deceleration |
| PPI | Monthly | Leading indicator of consumer inflation |
| NFP (Nonfarm Payrolls) | Monthly | Job creation vs. expectations; unemployment rate trend |
| PMI (Purchasing Managers Index) | Monthly | >50 = expansion; <50 = contraction; separates manufacturing vs. services |
| ISM Manufacturing | Monthly | Activity levels, new orders (forward-looking component) |
| GDP Growth Rate | Quarterly | Real vs. nominal; revisions |
| PCE Deflator | Monthly | Fed's preferred inflation gauge |
| Consumer Confidence (Conference Board) | Monthly | Leading indicator for consumer spending |
| Yield Curve (2/10 spread) | Daily | Inversion = recession warning |
| Credit Spreads (HY OAS) | Daily | Stress indicator for credit markets |
| Dollar Index (DXY) | Daily | Strong dollar hurts multinational earnings |

## Using Macro Data in Investment Decisions

The goal is not to predict macro but to:

1. **Understand the current regime** — growth/no growth, inflationary/deflationary, accommodative/restrictive
2. **Assess macro risk to your portfolio** — if rates rise 200bps, what happens to each position?
3. **Identify regime changes** — when the regime is shifting, sector rotation opportunities emerge
4. **Calibrate valuation assumptions** — WACC inputs, terminal growth rates, and sector multiples all depend on macro conditions

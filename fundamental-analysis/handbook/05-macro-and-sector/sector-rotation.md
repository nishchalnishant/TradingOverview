# Sector Rotation

Sector rotation is the process by which investment capital flows between economic sectors as macro conditions change. Different sectors outperform at different points in the economic and interest rate cycle. Understanding this creates opportunities to tilt exposure toward sectors with macro tailwinds.

---

## The Classic Sector Rotation Cycle

The standard model maps sector performance to the four stages of the economic cycle. It was popularized by Sam Stovall (Standard & Poor's) and is a widely taught but imperfectly consistent empirical pattern.

```
ECONOMIC CYCLE AND SECTOR PERFORMANCE

     EARLY CYCLE                    LATE CYCLE
   (Recovery, Rate Cuts)          (Peak, Rate Hikes)
   
   Financials ★★★               Energy ★★★
   Consumer Discretionary ★★★   Materials ★★
   Technology ★★                Industrials ★★
   Industrials ★★               Healthcare ★
   
        ↑ GDP bottoming                ↓ GDP peaking
   
   MID CYCLE                     RECESSION
   (Expansion)                   (Contraction)
   
   Technology ★★★               Consumer Staples ★★★
   Materials ★★                 Healthcare ★★★
   Energy ★★                    Utilities ★★★
   Industrials ★★               Telecom ★★
   Consumer Discretionary ★      Financials ✗
```

## Sector-by-Sector Characteristics

### Cyclical Sectors

These sectors are highly sensitive to GDP growth and the credit cycle. Their revenues expand and contract with the economy.

**Financials (Banks, Insurance, Asset Managers)**
- **Early/mid cycle:** Benefit from rising loan demand, recovering credit quality, expanding NIM as rates rise
- **Recession:** Hurt by credit losses, declining loan growth, flat/inverted yield curve compressing NIM
- **Key metrics:** Net Interest Margin, loan loss provisions, book value growth
- **Interest rate sensitivity:** Beneficiary of rising rates (asset-sensitive banks) until curve inverts

**Consumer Discretionary (Retail, Autos, Hotels, Restaurants)**
- **Early/mid cycle:** Consumer confidence high, employment strong, discretionary spending rises
- **Recession:** Consumers cut back on non-essential spending; highly cyclical revenues
- **Sub-sectors to distinguish:** Online retail (structural growth, less cyclical) vs. traditional retail; luxury goods (resilient due to wealthy customer base) vs. value retail (squeeze between inflation and cost pressure)
- **Key metrics:** Same-store sales, consumer confidence, credit card spending data

**Industrials (Manufacturing, Defense, Transportation, Construction)**
- **Mid/late cycle:** Capital spending by corporates and infrastructure investment peak later in the cycle
- **Recession:** Order backlogs drain; capital spending cut aggressively
- **Defense sub-sector:** Less cyclical; driven by geopolitical spending rather than the economy
- **Key metrics:** Purchasing Managers Index (PMI), industrial production, durable goods orders

**Materials (Metals, Mining, Chemicals, Paper)**
- **Late cycle:** Commodity prices typically peak late in expansions as demand is highest and supply is slow to respond
- **Recession:** Demand crashes; commodity prices fall sharply
- **Key metrics:** Commodity prices (copper, iron ore, aluminum), inventory levels, CRB index

**Energy (Oil & Gas)**
- **Late cycle/inflation peak:** Oil prices often peak in late expansion; energy companies profit from high commodity prices
- **Recession:** Oil demand falls; energy companies cut CapEx rapidly
- **Key metrics:** Crude oil price, natural gas price, E&P CapEx cycles, OPEC policy

**Technology (Hardware, Semiconductors)**
- **Early-to-mid cycle:** Capex-intensive hardware and semiconductors are early cycle (companies invest in tech upgrades early in recovery)
- **Software/SaaS:** More defensive than hardware; recurring subscription revenue smooths the cycle
- **Interest rate sensitivity:** High-multiple tech is long-duration equity; sensitive to rate increases
- **Key metrics:** Semiconductor book-to-bill ratio, PC/server shipment data, cloud growth rates

### Defensive Sectors

These sectors produce goods and services with inelastic demand — people buy them regardless of economic conditions.

**Consumer Staples (Food, Beverages, Tobacco, Household Products)**
- **All cycle:** Revenue relatively stable across cycles; people still buy food, toothpaste, beer
- **Recession:** Outperform cyclicals; investors rotate into "sleep-well-at-night" stocks
- **Rate sensitivity:** Some sensitivity as dividend-paying companies compete with bonds for income investors
- **Key metrics:** Organic volume growth, pricing vs. volume mix, private label competition

**Healthcare (Pharma, Med Devices, Hospitals, Health Insurance)**
- **All cycle:** Healthcare demand is non-discretionary; people don't delay cancer treatment in recessions
- **Recession:** Outperform; defensive demand, recurring revenues, limited price sensitivity for drugs/procedures
- **Sub-sectors:** Pharma (patent cliff risk, blockbuster concentration), biotech (binary trial risk), health insurers (Medical Loss Ratio regulation), hospitals (reimbursement pressure)
- **Key metrics:** Drug pipeline milestones, patent expiry schedules, MLR ratios, CMS reimbursement rates

**Utilities (Electric, Gas, Water)**
- **All cycle / recession:** Regulated monopolies with nearly guaranteed demand; the most bond-like equities
- **Rate sensitivity:** Most rate-sensitive sector. When long rates rise, utility dividend yields look less attractive by comparison; multiples compress. When rates fall, utilities outperform.
- **Key metrics:** Rate case filings and regulatory outcomes, capex plans for grid modernization, renewable energy transition

**Telecom (Wireless, Cable, Internet)**
- **Defensive:** Subscription-based services with high switching costs; relatively inelastic demand
- **Interest rate sensitivity:** Heavy debt loads make telecom rate-sensitive
- **Key metrics:** ARPU (average revenue per user), churn rate, capital intensity (5G buildout), cord-cutting trends

## Defensive vs. Cyclical vs. Growth: The Three Categories

| Category | Economic Sensitivity | Rate Sensitivity | Best Market Environments |
|----------|---------------------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| Defensive | Low | Moderate (dividend competition) | Recessions, slowdowns |
| Cyclical | High | Moderate | Early to mid expansion |
| Growth | Low-medium (earnings driven by secular, not cycle) | High (long duration equity) | Low-rate, high-growth macro |

**Growth sectors** (Technology, Consumer Discretionary high-growth sub-sectors, Healthcare innovation) are driven more by secular trends than economic cycles — but they are highly sensitive to interest rates because their multiples are based on distant future cash flows.

In a falling rate environment, growth stocks rally strongly. In a rising rate environment, they compress even with strong fundamentals — the discount rate compression hurts them disproportionately.

## Sector Rotation Signals

### Relative Strength Analysis

Track the ratio of sector ETF prices to the S&P 500 (e.g., XLK/SPY for technology). A rising ratio means the sector is outperforming; falling means underperforming. Sustained divergences signal rotation in or out.

**Momentum within sectors:** Sectors that have been leading for 3–6 months tend to continue leading until a clear macro regime change. Chasing the most recent 1-month outperformer is noisier.

### ETF Flows

Monitor weekly ETF fund flows by sector (available from Bloomberg, ETF.com, Morningstar):
- Sustained inflows into defensive ETFs (XLP, XLV, XLU) while cyclicals see outflows → market pricing in recession
- Flows into cyclicals and financials with outflows from defensives → rotation to risk-on

### Credit Markets as a Leading Indicator

High-yield credit spreads widen before equities sell off. Widening spreads signal increasing distress risk in the credit market — a leading indicator of equity weakness, particularly for highly leveraged sectors.

Investment-grade spreads widening moderately = slowdown concern. HY spreads spiking = credit crunch risk.

### Yield Curve Shape

- **Steep curve (long rates >> short rates):** Stimulative; banks benefit; early/mid cycle regime
- **Flat curve:** Late cycle; margins compressing; growth slowing
- **Inverted curve (short > long):** Historically precedes recessions by 6–18 months; rotate toward defensives

## Top-Down Investment Process

**Step 1: Assess the macro regime**
Where in the economic cycle are we? Is the Fed tightening or easing? Is inflation rising or falling? Use CPI, PMI, yield curve, credit spreads.

**Step 2: Identify favored sectors**
Given the current regime, which sectors have structural tailwinds? Which face headwinds?

**Step 3: Drill into industries within favored sectors**
Within the favored sector, which sub-industries have the best competitive structures? Use Porter's Five Forces.

**Step 4: Select individual stocks**
Within the favored industries, identify the companies with the best moats, management quality, and valuations.

**Step 5: Monitor for regime change signals**
The regime will change. Set alerts on yield curve shape, PMI inflections, Fed language shifts, and credit spread widening. Be prepared to rotate.

## Common Sector Rotation Mistakes

**1. Timing the rotation too precisely**
Macro regime changes take 6–18 months to fully materialize. Rotating too early means underperforming for months. Rotating too late means you've missed the move.

**2. Ignoring company-specific quality for macro themes**
Buying low-quality cyclical companies just because you're in an "early cycle" regime is a mistake. Within any sector, quality companies outperform over time. Never abandon company quality assessment for macro themes.

**3. Over-rotating (churning)**
Transaction costs, taxes, and the difficulty of timing macro precisely mean that aggressive rotation destroys value for most investors. Use macro context to set tilts, not to make binary switches.

**4. Confusing secular with cyclical**
Some sectors have both cyclical and secular components. Technology hardware is cyclical; cloud software has secular growth drivers. Don't assume the entire technology sector rotates together.

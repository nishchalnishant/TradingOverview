# Earnings Season Playbook

Earnings season (four times per year) is when companies report quarterly results against analyst expectations. It's the highest-volume period for stock price moves — individual stocks can move 5–20%+ in a single session after reporting. Understanding how to position before, read the report, and react after is a critical skill for both short-term and long-term investors.

---

## The Earnings Calendar Cycle

**Earnings season timing (US):**
- Q1 (January–March) reports: April–May
- Q2 (April–June) reports: July–August
- Q3 (July–September) reports: October–November
- Q4 (October–December) reports: January–February (following year)

Large-cap companies (S&P 500) report within 2–4 weeks of quarter end. Smaller companies may take 5–6 weeks.

**Check earnings dates:** Use Earnings Whispers, EDGAR, investor relations pages, or Bloomberg. Set a calendar 2–3 weeks before critical positions report.

## Pre-Announcement Positioning

### Consensus Estimates

**The Whisper Number vs. The Consensus:**
Published analyst consensus (visible on Bloomberg, FactSet, Yahoo Finance) is what the official estimate says. But the "whisper number" — the unspoken expectation the sophisticated buy-side is actually using — can differ significantly.

If consensus EPS is $1.00 but the whisper is $1.10, a report of $1.05 (a "beat" vs. consensus) will feel like a miss to the market. This explains seemingly paradoxical reactions where stocks fall despite beating official estimates.

**How to estimate the whisper:**
- Track the pattern of analyst estimate revisions in the 2–4 weeks before the report. Systematic upward revisions signal buy-side consensus is moving higher than official published consensus.
- Compare historical "beat rate" — if the company beats by 5%+ every quarter, the market expects a beat and prices it in. A 2% beat may disappoint.

**EPS vs. Revenue Beats:**
Both matter, but revenue beats are typically better-received than pure EPS beats (which could be driven by buybacks or tax changes). A revenue miss with EPS beat is usually punished.

### IV Spike in Options Before Earnings

Options implied volatility (IV) expands before earnings announcements as traders buy options to position for the move. IV typically collapses immediately after the announcement — this is **IV crush**.

**Pre-earnings IV dynamics:**
- IV typically rises 30–100%+ relative to normal in the week before earnings
- The options market prices an "implied move" (the expected magnitude of the post-earnings stock move): `Implied Move ≈ ATM Straddle Price / Stock Price`
- Example: If a $100 stock has an ATM straddle (call + put at $100 strike) priced at $8, the implied move is ~8%

**Historical move vs. implied move:**
Compare the stock's average actual post-earnings move (over last 4–8 quarters) to the current implied move. If historical average is 5% and the implied move is 10%, IV may be overpriced — selling premium (straddles, strangles) could be profitable. If historical is 12% and implied is 8%, buying premium (long straddle) may offer edge.

### Pre-Earnings Research Prep

Before any earnings report on a position:

1. **Know the consensus:** EPS, revenue, gross margin, operating margin. Know the range (not just the mean).
2. **Identify the key metric:** What does the market care most about? For SaaS: Net Revenue Retention, ARR, operating margin inflection. For retail: same-store sales, gross margin. For banks: NIM, credit quality.
3. **Read the last 4 earnings transcripts:** What was management saying? What problems were flagged? Were they solved?
4. **Check the options market's implied move** — don't be surprised by the post-earnings move magnitude.
5. **Assess the sentiment setup:** Is the stock up 30% into earnings (high bar), flat (neutral bar), or down 30% (low bar)?

## Reading the Earnings Report

### Structure of Earnings Day

1. **Earnings release (press release):** Usually released pre-market (6–7 AM) or after-market (4–5 PM). Contains headline EPS, revenue, guidance. The stock moves most on this release.
2. **Earnings call:** Typically 30–60 minutes after the release. CEO/CFO present results, then Q&A with analysts.
3. **10-Q filing:** Full quarterly report filed within 40 days of quarter end. Contains detailed financial statements and footnotes.

### What to Analyze in the Release

**Headline metrics — check first:**
- EPS: Beat, miss, or in-line vs. consensus? By how much?
- Revenue: Beat, miss, or in-line? Organic vs. total growth?
- Gross margin: Expanding, contracting, or stable?

**Guidance — this often drives the reaction more than the results:**
- Did guidance raise, maintain, or lower?
- Is the raise "large enough" relative to what was priced in?
- Full-year vs. next-quarter guidance divergence?

**"Beat-and-Raise" is the ideal result:**
- Beat on current quarter metrics AND raise full-year guidance
- This signals management confidence in business momentum
- Strong beat-and-raise typically produces the largest sustained price moves

**"Miss-and-Lower" is the worst result:**
- Miss current quarter AND guide down for the full year
- Signals deteriorating business conditions
- Typically produces significant sell-off; the question is whether the new, lower guidance fully resets expectations

**"Beat-and-In-line" (beat current, maintain guidance):**
- Mixed reaction; market was hoping for a raise
- May signal that Q1 beat was one-time, or that management is being conservative

**"In-line-and-Raise" (meet quarter, raise guidance):**
- Often well-received if the raise is meaningful — signals management confidence without depending on a big current beat

### Earnings Call Analysis

**Management tone and language:**
Experienced earnings call listeners learn to read qualitative signals that don't appear in the numbers:

| Positive Signals | Negative Signals |
|----------------|----------------|
| Specific, quantified commentary on demand | Vague language: "We remain cautious but optimistic" |
| Unprompted disclosure of good news | Burying problems in the prepared remarks |
| Raising guidance by more than the Q beat | Guidance raise exactly matches the beat (no real raise) |
| Confident body language in Q&A | Short, evasive answers to analyst questions |
| Management buying back stock at current levels | Insiders selling heading into the report |
| New products/customers discussed with specifics | Announcing strategic reviews (often a distress signal) |

**Listen for:**
- Are analysts asking the same concern across multiple questions? (Signals a known problem)
- Does management avoid quantifying a key metric they previously disclosed? (Hiding deterioration)
- Is the CEO/CFO making excuses or taking accountability?

**The analyst Q&A:**
The prepared remarks are scripted and carefully crafted. The Q&A is where real information surfaces. Analysts (who talk to management, competitors, and customers regularly) probe known concerns. Watch for management deflection.

## Beat-and-Raise vs. Miss-and-Lower: Market Reactions

### Why Markets React Asymmetrically

Losses hurt twice as much as equivalent gains feel good (loss aversion). This means:
- Miss-and-lower stocks often fall more than the fundamental change in value suggests
- Beat-and-raise stocks often rise immediately but may consolidate as the move is partially given back

**The "Sell the News" Phenomenon:**
Stocks that have rallied significantly into earnings often sell off even on a beat, because the good news was already priced in. The beat met expectations but provided no positive surprise relative to what the market was already pricing.

**Oversold on a miss:**
A company with a strong moat that reports a minor miss (e.g., supply chain delay, one weak region) often oversells on the announcement day, creating a buying opportunity for patient investors who know the business. The fundamental value hasn't changed; sentiment has temporarily overreacted.

## Post-Earnings Drift (PEAD)

**PEAD (Post-Earnings Announcement Drift)** is one of the most well-documented anomalies in financial markets. It was first documented by Ball and Brown (1968) and has persisted for decades.

**The Pattern:**
Stocks that report positive earnings surprises tend to continue drifting upward over the subsequent 30–90 days. Stocks that report negative surprises continue drifting downward.

**Why it exists:**
- Investor attention is limited; not all information is processed on announcement day
- Institutional investors take time to update models and communicate internally
- Some investors wait to see whether the positive surprise persists before buying
- Analyst estimate revisions follow slowly after the report, creating a slow-moving wave of upgrades/downgrades

**Magnitude:**
PEAD studies show the largest drift in the most extreme decile of surprises — the biggest beats and biggest misses. Moderate beats/misses show less drift.

**How to use PEAD:**
- After a large positive earnings surprise (top decile), a portion of the upside move may come over the next 30–60 trading days, not just on announcement day
- After a large miss, the stock may continue underperforming for 30–60 days as estimates are revised down
- Don't assume the entire move happens on day 1

**Caveats:**
- PEAD is smaller in large-cap, highly liquid, analyst-covered stocks (information is processed faster)
- Has diminished over time as algorithmic traders exploit it more efficiently
- The drift is in the same direction as the surprise only about 60–65% of the time — not a reliable short-term trade on its own

## Avoiding IV Crush with Options Around Earnings

**IV crush** is the rapid collapse of implied volatility immediately after an earnings report. IV that was elevated (pricing uncertainty) deflates once the uncertainty resolves.

**The IV crush problem for option buyers:**
If you buy a call option before earnings, you pay for elevated IV. After the report, even if the stock moves in your favor, the collapse of IV reduces the option's value significantly. The stock might move +5% but your call might barely break even because IV fell 40%.

**Strategies to manage IV crush:**

**1. Long spread (debit spread)**
Buy a call/put spread instead of naked options. Both legs suffer from IV crush, so the net impact is reduced.
- Example: Instead of buying the $100 call for $4, buy the $100/$110 call spread for $2.50. Less premium at risk; lower breakeven.

**2. Use the implied move as a guide**
If implied move is 8% and you only expect the stock to move 5% in your favor, avoid long options. If you expect a 15% move (significantly above implied), the IV crush doesn't matter — the directional move outweighs it.

**3. Pre-earnings, not on-earnings positioning**
Enter directional positions 1–2 weeks before earnings when IV is still rising but not yet at peak. Exit before the report if you're trading IV expansion rather than direction.

**4. Selling premium (for neutral view)**
If you believe the implied move overestimates actual volatility (stock will move less than priced), sell premium: short straddle, strangle, or iron condor. You benefit from IV crush as long as the stock stays within the implied move range. Risk: if the stock makes a large unexpected move, losses are significant.

**5. Risk-defined credit spreads**
Sell iron condors or iron butterflies to collect premium while capping downside. Define your max loss before the trade.

## Earnings Season Checklist

**Pre-earnings (1–2 weeks before):**
- [ ] Confirm earnings date and time
- [ ] Note consensus EPS, revenue, key metric estimates
- [ ] Calculate implied move from ATM straddle price
- [ ] Compare implied move to historical average move
- [ ] Check for any pre-announcements or guidance revisions
- [ ] Review last quarter's call for pending issues management was monitoring
- [ ] Assess sentiment setup (stock up/down into earnings)

**On earnings day:**
- [ ] Read the press release for headline metrics and guidance
- [ ] Compare EPS/Revenue/Gross Margin vs. consensus
- [ ] Assess guidance: beat-and-raise, maintain, or cut?
- [ ] Listen to the call; note management tone in Q&A
- [ ] Identify the key metric the market reacts to most
- [ ] Check whether the initial reaction makes sense given the fundamentals

**Post-earnings (days 1–30):**
- [ ] Are analyst estimates being revised? In which direction?
- [ ] Is the stock drifting in the PEAD direction?
- [ ] If it was a miss: has the selloff created a buying opportunity, or is the business genuinely impaired?
- [ ] Update your investment thesis: what did you learn about the business that you didn't know before?
